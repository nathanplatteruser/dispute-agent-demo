# What To Do Monday

Three things. All of them you can start this week without buying anything
or hiring anyone.

---

## 1. Pull 50 of your own letters and read them as the consumer

Take 50 dispute response letters your shop sent in the last 90 days.
Do not read them as a compliance officer. Read them as the person who
opened the envelope.

For each letter, answer these three questions:

- Does the letter tell the consumer exactly what to do next, in plain
  language, within the first two paragraphs?
- Does the letter contain any phrase the consumer would need a law degree
  to understand? ("Pursuant to," "notwithstanding," "hereinafter" — mark
  every one.)
- If someone handed you this letter and you owed $3,000 you were not
  sure about, would you call the number on it, or would you put it in
  a drawer?

You will find a pattern. The letters that generate callbacks and
resolution will read differently from the ones that generate silence.
Track which is which. That data is worth more than any AI tool.

---

## 2. Compare your dispute categories to what consumers actually say

Pull your dispute classification categories — however you bucket them
today. Then pull 100 actual consumer complaints or call transcripts.

Read the consumer's words. Ask: does your category system match the
language consumers use, or does it match the language your internal
systems use?

Most shops classify disputes by what the system needs (account type,
balance range, legal status). Consumers classify disputes by what
happened to them (someone stole my identity, I already paid this,
the amount is wrong). When those two systems do not align, letters
go to the wrong template, the wrong tone, or the wrong department.

You do not need AI for this. You need a spreadsheet and two columns:
"What the consumer said" and "What we called it." Where those diverge
is where your process is leaking.

---

## 3. Add one fact-check to your letter process

Before any letter goes out, have someone (or something) answer one
question: does every factual claim in this letter appear in the source
record?

If the letter says "your payment of $500 was received on March 15th,"
is there a $500 payment on March 15th in the account record? If the
letter says "the original balance was $2,400," does the record show
$2,400?

This is what the review gate in the demo pipeline does. You do not
need to build software to start. Print a letter, print the account
screen, and put them side by side. Circle any claim in the letter that
is not on the screen. That is your hallucination rate, whether the
letter was written by a person or a machine.

Start with five letters a day. It takes about two minutes per letter.
Within a week, you will know whether your current process has a
fact-accuracy problem, and you will have specific examples to show
your team.

---

These three actions share a common thread: look at what you are already
sending through the eyes of the person receiving it. The tooling matters
less than the habit.
